Compliance, Defiance, and the Illusion of Agency: Where Is the Line Drawn?

Vageesh Dwivedi • September 25, 2026 • 8 min read

We all like to believe that our moral compass is immutable. In hypothetical ethics seminars, nearly everyone insists they would refuse an unlawful command, speak truth to power, and stand firm when a superior asks them to cross an ethical line.

Yet behavioral history paints a far more disturbing reality. Under the weight of perceived legitimate authority, human moral agency does not simply bend—it fractures. When corporate hierarchies, social conformity, and authoritative pressure collide, ordinary, well-meaning professionals routinely surrender their autonomy to become instruments of harm.

The Core Question: Where does legitimate organizational obedience end and moral complicity begin? When an authority figure demands compliance, who truly owns the consequences—the person giving the order, or the person executing the keystroke?

The Mount Washington McDonald's Incident: Authority Bias in the Wild

On April 9, 2004, at a McDonald's restaurant in Mount Washington, Kentucky, a call came through to the store manager. The caller identified himself as "Detective Scott" from the local police department. He claimed that an 18-year-old female employee working the counter had just stolen a customer's purse and had drugs hidden in her clothes.

Over the next three and a half hours, without ever displaying a badge, producing a warrant, or even being physically present, a disembodied voice over a crackling telephone line orchestrated an escalating nightmare.

The caller ordered the assistant manager to bring the teenager into a back office, confiscate her phone, conduct an invasive strip search, and force her to perform demeaning physical tasks. When the assistant manager had to return to the front counter, she handed the phone to her fiancé, who was instructed by the "detective" to conduct further degrading and abusive acts against the crying teenager.

The caller was not a police officer. He was David Stewart, a private security guard who had placed dozens of similar hoax calls across the United States.

What makes this case one of the most chilling psychological case studies in modern history is not the depravity of the hoaxer; it is that none of the people in that room were violent predators. They were ordinary, law-abiding citizens. Yet, under the psychological spell of an authoritative voice citing corporate headquarters, using police jargon, and threatening legal repercussions, their personal agency evaporated.

The Role of Agency: Milgram’s "Agentic State"

How does an otherwise moral person participate in profound harm without actively intending it?

Social psychologist Stanley Milgram provided the scientific framework following his famous 1961 Yale obedience experiments. Milgram observed that when an individual enters a recognized hierarchy, they undergo an Agentic Shift:

"The person entering an authority system no longer views himself as acting out of his own purposes but rather comes to view himself as an agent for executing the wishes of another person."
— Stanley Milgram, Obedience to Authority (1974)

In the agentic state, a person's conscience is not disabled; rather, its orientation is fundamentally redirected:

  • Autonomous State: The individual feels personally responsible for the morality and human consequences of their actions.
  • Agentic State: The individual relinquishes moral responsibility to the authority figure. Their moral pride is now derived entirely from competence, loyalty, and procedural obedience to the superior.

In the McDonald's tragedy, the manager did not believe strip-searching an employee was morally right. Rather, she believed that refusing a detective's direct order was insubordination. She relinquished her agency to the uniform on the telephone.

The Ethical Triad: Legal vs. Moral vs. Ethical Compliance

To understand where the line between obedience and defiance must be drawn, we must disentangle three distinct spheres:

Sphere Defining Authority Operating Criterion The Failure Mode
Legal Compliance Statutes, Courts, Terms of Service "Is it explicitly forbidden by law?" Legalistic cynicism; legality does not equal morality.
Ethical Compliance Industry Standards, Codes (ACM, IEEE) "Does it honor professional duties & contracts?" Check-the-box audits; bureaucratic compliance theatre.
Moral Agency Individual Conscience & Human Dignity "Does this action inflict harm or deceit on human beings?" Requires courage; carries social and career risks.

Historically, the most devastating institutional failures were strictly "legal" according to the statutes of the organizations enforcing them. When corporate policies or executive commands demand actions that degrade human dignity or falsify reality, compliance ceases to be a virtue and becomes complicity.

The Parallel to Enterprise Software and Tech Hierarchies

It is easy to dismiss the McDonald’s incident as an extreme aberration in a fast-food franchise. But the software industry—particularly enterprise software and large corporate engineering organizations—is extraordinarily vulnerable to the very same psychological pathology.

Consider the structural realities of modern enterprise engineering:

1. The Abstraction of Physical Distance

In physical industries, hurting someone produces immediate sensory feedback: screams, tears, or blood. In software, moral harm is mediated through cold abstractions: code commits, pull requests, microservices, and databases.

An engineer who writes a routine to silently scrape user location data, train models on non-consensual biometric inputs, or throttle healthcare claims algorithms never sees the person on the other end. This psychological distance accelerates the agentic shift.

2. The "Just a Jira Ticket" Defense

How many catastrophic tech failures begin with the phrase: "I just implemented what was in the user story"?

  • Volkswagen’s Dieselgate: Software engineers programmed engine control units (ECUs) to detect when the vehicle was undergoing laboratory emissions testing and artificially reduce toxic NOx emissions, reverting to 40x illegal pollution levels on real roads. When investigated, engineers admitted they knew it was deceptive, but felt they had no choice: executives had set impossible targets, and engineering culture demanded compliance.
  • Boeing 737 MAX (MCAS): Software engineers and mid-level managers were acutely aware of single-point-of-failure vulnerabilities in angle-of-attack sensors. Yet intense managerial pressure to avoid costly simulator training mandates and meet production milestones suppressed internal dissent until two aircraft crashed, killing 346 people.
  • Enterprise Employee Surveillance Tools: During the remote work boom, enterprise software teams engineered intrusive keystroke loggers, webcam screen-snappers, and automated "productivity scores." Developers routinely expressed private disgust, but justified their work under executive mandate: "The enterprise customer demanded this feature; if we don't build it, a competitor will."

3. Hierarchical Diffusion of Responsibility

In a large enterprise (Junior Engineer → Staff Architect → Engineering Manager → Director → VP → Legal Counsel → C-Suite), responsibility is sliced into microscopic increments.

The developer assumes Legal vetted it. Legal assumes Product scoped it ethically. Product assumes the Executive team approved it. The Executive assumes the Board demands growth. Because everyone is an agent, no single human feels the moral weight of the outcome.

4. The Weaponized "C-Suite Proxy": Heavy Titles in the Absence of Data

Perhaps the most ubiquitous manifestation of the McDonald's hoax dynamic in corporate software is the weaponized C-level invocation.

In organizations around the globe, critical architectural and ethical decisions are railroaded through meetings with a single phrase:

"Hey, I talked to the CTO this morning, and we need to push this through right now."
"The CEO explicitly asked for this feature by end of month—no debates."

Just like the voice on the telephone claiming to be "Detective Scott," invoking heavy titles operates as an epistemic silencer. It short-circuits rational debate, shuts down empirical analysis, and renders data irrelevant. When an ambitious manager or technical lead wants to steamroll objections about technical debt, missing security gates, or ethical red flags, they simply drape themselves in the mantle of executive authority.

The Vulnerability of Junior Engineers: This dynamic is especially predatory toward junior developers, interns, and newer hires. Fresh engineers possess neither the institutional leverage nor the political capital to challenge a perceived executive decree.

Worse, most junior employees do not even realize what they are agreeing to do. They innocently assume: "If the CTO or VP asked for this, senior leadership must have already weighed the legal liability, security risks, and user impact."

In reality, the junior engineer is often instructed to write a script that silently bypasses authentication, disables encryption during database migrations, strips data privacy consent checks (violating GDPR or HIPAA), or implements deceptive dark patterns. Unbeknownst to them, they have been maneuvered into becoming the sole author of an illegal or disastrous git commit, while the manager who invoked the executive title leaves no paper trail.

Furthermore, in 80% of these cases, the "Executive Telephone Game" is at play. The CEO or CTO may have simply made an offhand, hypothetical comment over coffee—"Wouldn't it be interesting if we could track user journeys in real-time?"—which an overzealous manager warped into an emergency mandate to install surveillance telemetry without consent.

Where Is the Line Drawn? The Duty of "Intelligent Defiance"

If obedience to authority is our default social programming, how do engineering organizations and individuals cultivate the capacity to push back before catastrophe strikes?

The answer lies in a concept borrowed from service dog training and aviation: Intelligent Disobedience.

A guide dog is trained to obey commands like "forward" or "cross." However, if a train is approaching the platform or an open manhole lies in the path, the dog is trained to actively refuse the command. The dog exercises situational defiance to preserve the life of its human partner.

In aviation, Crew Resource Management (CRM) was mandated after multiple fatal crashes where first officers recognized a fatal error by a senior captain, but were too intimidated by cockpit hierarchy to intervene. CRM explicitly created a legal and procedural mandate for junior officers to challenge their superiors.

Actionable Principles for Software Leaders and Engineers

1. Establish Inviolable Architectural Redlines

Every engineering team must define ethical boundaries before high-stakes pressure arrives. If a directive involves falsifying test results, bypassing data privacy consent without lawful cause, silencing security vulnerabilities, or weaponizing dark patterns to trap vulnerable users, defiance is not optional—it is a non-negotiable professional obligation.

2. Force In-Writing Escalation

When pressured by authority to compromise ethical or safety standards, convert verbal pressure into documented records:

"Per our discussion, I have raised concerns that disabling this data validation check risks exposing customer PII in violation of GDPR Article 32. You have instructed me to bypass this check to meet the Friday launch deadline. Please confirm your approval of this risk acceptance in writing so we can document the decision."

Documenting decisions forces the superior out of vague informal pressure and holds them directly accountable for the trade-off. In many cases, the demand instantly evaporates when personal attribution is required.

3. Protect the Dissenter Structurally

If saying "no" to an unethical directive requires an engineer to risk their job, mortgage, or career, your organization does not have an ethics problem—it has a toxic leadership structure.

Leadership must create anonymous reporting channels, explicit ombudsman pathways, and independent architectural review boards empowered to halt releases without fear of executive retaliation.

4. Neutralize the "C-Suite Proxy" (The Title-to-Data Conversion Rule)

When an intermediary insists that "XYZ executive told us to do this," apply two non-negotiable operational rules:

  • Close the Loop in the Open: Confirm directly on the public RFC or ticket: "Hi [CTO], [Manager] mentioned you requested we bypass this security token validation for the Q3 release. Before proceeding, here are the architectural failure modes and GDPR implications. Do you approve accepting this risk?" Fraudulent or distorted proxy mandates dissolve almost instantaneously under direct sunshine.
  • Protect Junior Engineers with Informed Consent: Never assign a junior engineer to write code that modifies privacy, authentication, or compliance controls without a senior engineer walking them through the systemic implications. Junior developers must be trained that asking "What is the data supporting this?" is not insubordination—it is professional excellence.

Conclusion: The Courage of Moral Ownership

An organizational chart is an administrative tool, not an ethical shield.

When you write software, design systems, or lead teams, you are not merely an agent of an enterprise; you are an autonomous moral actor whose technical decisions ripple into the real lives of human beings.

Compliance maintains order, but intelligent defiance preserves our humanity. When the voice on the telephone—or the executive in the boardroom—demands that you violate basic ethics in the name of authority, remembering that you always retain your agency is the ultimate test of leadership.

In an industry that routinely rewards passive acquiescence and hiding behind Jira tickets, the greatest architectural safeguard is not another static analysis tool or compliance committee—it is the moral fortitude to push back against destructive orders. Grow the spine.
