Building Trust Within Your Engineering Team

Vageesh Dwivedi • March 20, 2018 • 5 min read

In traditional command-and-control organizations, employees were told what to do without being trusted with the "why." But modern technology architectures are far too complex for leadership to do all the thinking alone. High-velocity engineering requires shared consciousness and empowered execution—and the foundational bedrock is trust.

The Two Dimensions of Trust

  • Benevolence-Based Trust: Built on emotional bonds, genuine empathy, shared values, and mutual respect. Do your colleagues believe that you care about their well-being?
  • Competence-Based Trust: Rooted in technical capability, reliability, follow-through, and engineering execution. When you commit to delivering an API or fixing a critical blocker, do you deliver?

What Happens When Trust Breaks Down

When trust deteriorates within an engineering organization, toxic symptoms emerge: engineers hoard pertinent system knowledge, meetings start late, code reviews become passive-aggressive, and managers default to micromanagement and surveillance.

How to Repair Broken Trust

  1. Admit, Apologize, and Ask: Acknowledge where the misstep occurred without defensiveness.
  2. Ask Questions Instead of Making Statements: Rather than saying "Your service architecture is wrong," ask: "I'm finding it difficult to understand how this interface handles failovers. Could you walk me through your thinking?"
  3. Relinquish Surveillance: Trust allows you to give up rigid control. Checking whether an engineer is active on Slack should never be the job of a leader in a modern engineering culture.

"The best way to find out if you can trust somebody is to trust them." — Ernest Hemingway

Key Leadership Takeaways

Build an explicit engineering manifesto that codifies your architectural values. Create psychological safety where team members feel empowered to say "I made a mistake" or "I don't know yet, but I'll find out." When failure is safe, innovation flourishes.
