Taking Over a New Engineering Team or Organization

Vageesh Dwivedi • July 18, 2018 • 8 min read

Leaders today have an average of 18.2 years of professional work experience. Within these years, you are typically promoted 4.1 times, move between business functions 1.8 times, and join a new company 3–5 times. Conclusion: Taking over a team will happen for sure!

Advantages and Pitfalls

Taking over a team is an incredible opportunity to start fresh and make high-leverage changes. However, it can also be the most challenging period in a leader's career because:

  • You have no established working relationships with new team members.
  • You start with little or no context of the historic team baggage or architecture.
  • You are subject to an unusually high degree of scrutiny. Opinions of you form remarkably quickly with very little data.

Two Primary Scenarios

1. When You Are Promoted to Take Over a Team: You already understand the organization, but must develop the behaviors and competencies required at new levels. You have a broader scope and surface area—you must balance depth and breadth, drilling into one or two critical areas without losing the big picture. Delegate more deeply; casual conversations no longer scale, so communicate more formally with tools like all-hands.

2. When You Join a New Organization or Company: These are often lateral moves, but you need to adjust to an unfamiliar organizational context, structure, and culture. Think of yourself as an organ being donated into another body: if you do not adapt, you will be attacked by its immune system! Build context, build relationships, align expectations, and genuinely understand the culture before attempting major shake-ups.

The Trust Equation

Trustworthiness (T) = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) / Self-Orientation

Show that you care about other people's needs and their agenda. When team members realize you are invested in their success, trust flourishes naturally.

Securing Early Wins

Early wins build your credibility and create immediate organizational momentum. Focus on business priorities and identify quick wins that help advance your long-term goals while shaping the culture you need.

Driving Change: The 5 Pillars

Before introducing any systemic change, ensure these five pillars exist: Awareness, Diagnosis, Vision, Plan, and Support. If any pillar is missing, pause and invest in collective learning through off-sites or collaborative brainstorming.

The 30 / 60 / 90 Day Blueprint

  • First 30 Days: Focus entirely on building relationships and listening.
  • First 60 Days: Map out the deep technical and operational context.
  • First 90 Days: You must have tangible value shipped to establish momentum.

What to Do When You Inherit a Mess

A team may be deeply flawed while individual engineers are talented. If an injured team is suffering from lack of leadership or overwhelm, heal that injury. If a team structure is fundamentally unviable, ensure each member is transitioned to a place where they can win, and restructure.
