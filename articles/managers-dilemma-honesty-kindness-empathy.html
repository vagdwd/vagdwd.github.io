The Manager's Dilemma: Balancing Honesty, Kindness, and Empathy

Vageesh Dwivedi • February 24, 2025 • 6 min read

For years, I wrestled with a fundamental question: Should a manager prioritize honesty or kindness in conversations? I believed that being brutally honest could build trust, but sometimes, it led to discomfort. On the other hand, kindness made conversations easier, but at times, it felt like I wasn’t being fully transparent.

It wasn’t until recently that I realized the answer lay in neither honesty nor kindness alone. The missing piece was empathy—the ability to be both honest and kind, in a way that meets the emotional and psychological needs of the other person.

The Moment of Realization

Curious about how my team perceived me, I reached out to a few employees and asked: "What feeling comes to mind when you think of me?" One employee's response stood out:

"The first thing that comes to mind is your honesty in conversations. You’ve always been direct about the current state of things, without sugarcoating, which has helped me stay grounded and aware of reality."

Was I truly honest? Or had I just been direct? Honesty without empathy can feel harsh. Empathy ensures that honesty is received as helpful, not hurtful. I recall one instance where aiming for directness, I bluntly pointed out flaws in an employee's proposal during a team meeting. While my intention was to be helpful and honest, the employee felt publicly criticized and demotivated. It was a clear example of honesty backfiring due to a lack of empathy.

The Science of Conversations

Harvard professor Alison Wood Brooks studies the psychology of conversations and developed the T.A.L.K. framework:

  • Topics: Choosing the right things to talk about.
  • Asking: Engaging through meaningful questions.
  • Levity: Using warmth and humor to ease tension.
  • Kindness: Showing genuine care in interactions.

She emphasizes the microdecision-making that happens in every conversation—small choices about when to speak, how much to share, and how to respond to emotions. These decisions determine whether a conversation builds trust or erodes it.

A common pitfall is "boomerasking"—where conversations become self-centered.

Example of Boomerasking

Imagine a manager in a one-on-one meeting with an employee:

Employee: "I’ve been feeling overwhelmed with the new project deadlines. It’s been hard to keep up."

Manager: "Yeah, I totally get it! Back when I was handling projects like this, I had to work late nights and weekends to stay on track. It was tough, but I managed. You’ll get through it too!"

Instead of acknowledging the employee’s concerns, the manager redirects the conversation to their own experience—missing an opportunity to offer real support. A more empathetic response would be:

Manager: "That sounds really challenging. What aspects of the project are causing the most stress for you? Let’s see if we can adjust priorities or find additional resources to help."

Another challenge, highlighted by Rachel Greenwald, is the "zero-questioner"—someone who talks but never asks questions. This "zero-questioner" approach often stems from a lack of cognitive empathy – the failure to consider the other person's perspective and engage with their thoughts. Research also suggests that 24% of the time, our minds wander during conversations, which can make people feel unheard.

This explained why some of my interactions felt transactional rather than meaningful. I was communicating, but was I truly connecting?

The Role of Empathy in Conversations

To build stronger connections, I needed to move beyond honesty and kindness and embrace empathy—a concept that involves more than just understanding feelings.

The Three Types of Empathy, as illustrated in the image below, highlight how real empathy works:

  1. Cognitive Empathy (I put myself in your shoes): Understanding another person’s perspective.
  2. Emotional Empathy (I feel WITH you): Feeling the emotions they are experiencing.
  3. Compassionate Empathy (I will stand with you, without fixing): Offering support without trying to control or solve everything.

To integrate this into leadership, I began responding with empathy in a four-part-approach:

  1. Acknowledging emotions – "I can see that this situation has been frustrating for you."
  2. Demonstrating understanding – "Given what you've shared, I understand why this feels unfair."
  3. Validating experiences – "It makes sense that you’d feel this way given the challenges you've faced."
  4. Offering support – "Let’s work together to find a solution that helps you move forward."

Measuring Empathy

Empathy measurement is a science in itself, and the data points involved are both important and complex. Understanding the nuances of empathy measurement is crucial for leaders because it allows for self-awareness and the refinement of empathetic approaches. There are two closely related but distinct concepts of empathy:

  1. The Affective State of Empathy – This refers to an individual's personal experience of empathy. If you ask someone, “Did you feel like you knew how this person felt?”, you are measuring their affective state of empathy.
  2. The Ability to Empathize – This measures how accurately a person understands another's emotions and situation. If you assess whether Person A fully understood Person B, you are measuring their ability to empathize.

While these are closely related, they are still mutually exclusive concepts. Someone can feel empathy towards another person and still be completely wrong about what the person is feeling or why. However, we would still say that they are experiencing a state of empathy.

This realization was a turning point for me. I might have been empathizing with someone, but was I truly understanding their experience? Until their state of mind and feelings are fully understood and acknowledged, my empathy might have been directed at something completely unrelated to their experience. My four-part approach, while well-intentioned, could fall flat if I wasn't hitting the right emotional note.

Bridging the Gap: Ensuring Accurate Empathy

So, how do we bridge this gap between feeling empathy and accurately understanding another person's experience? Here are some practical strategies:

  • Active Listening: Truly listen to what the other person is saying, both verbally and nonverbally. Pay attention to their tone of voice, body language, and facial expressions.
  • Clarifying Questions: Don't be afraid to ask questions to ensure you're understanding correctly. So, if I understand correctly, you're feeling X because of Y?
  • Reflecting: Summarize what you've heard to confirm your understanding. "It sounds like you're saying..."
  • Seeking Feedback: Ask the other person if you've understood them correctly. Have I captured what you're feeling?
  • Mindfulness of Bias: Be aware of your own biases and assumptions, and how they might be coloring your perception of the other person's experience.

The Leadership Shift: From Honesty vs. Kindness to Empathy

Managers often struggle between being honest and being kind, but this is a false choice. Empathy allows us to be both. By understanding how conversations work, recognizing microdecisions, and practicing the Three Types of Empathy, we can create workplaces where honesty feels supportive, not harsh—and kindness feels genuine, not sugarcoated. And do measure the quality and accuracy as well as a self-reflection.

The next time you find yourself in a difficult conversation, ask yourself: Am I just being honest, or am I being empathetically honest? That small shift can transform relationships and leadership effectiveness.
